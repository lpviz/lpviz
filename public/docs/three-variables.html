The 3-variable editor

At lpviz.net/3d the decision variable has three coordinates. The feasible region is a convex solid instead of a polygon, every face is one inequality constraint, and the same five solvers run on the resulting LP — with their paths drawn as tubes through space rather than lines on a plane.

The problem

Nothing about the LP changes except n: maximize cᵀx subject to Ax ≤ b with x ∈ ℝ³. Each row of Ax ≤ b is now a half-space, so the feasible region is the intersection of half-spaces — a convex polyhedron — and each of its faces is exactly one constraint. The panel at the top of the sidebar lists that system as you build, one inequality per face, in the same form the 2-variable app uses.

The editor keeps the solid convex by construction: what you place are its corners, and the solid is always their convex hull. Any edit that would fold a face inward simply produces the hull of the new corners, so the constraint system always describes a genuine convex region.

Building the solid

Modeling proceeds in three short phases, then stays editable.

  1. Sketch the base. Click the grid to add the vertices of a base polygon on the ground plane, exactly as in the 2-variable app. Close it by clicking the first vertex or pressing Enter.
  2. Extrude. An orange handle appears at the base's center. Drag it upward to pull the polygon into a prism; the height is the extrusion.
  3. Aim the objective. Move the mouse to aim the objective arrow, anchored at the solid's centroid, and click to lock it in. From here the solvers run and every edit re-solves live.

Once the solid exists you sculpt it directly:

  • Move a corner by dragging it. Corners move on the plane facing the camera, so a drag tracks the mouse from any viewpoint.
  • Split a face: double-click it to plant a new vertex on it, then pull that vertex out along the face's normal. The hull grows a new corner and the old face becomes several.
  • Push or pull a face by dragging it; it slides along its own normal, which is how you tighten or relax one constraint.
  • Cut a corner by double-clicking it: a new face chamfers it away.
  • Bevel an edge by double-clicking it.
  • Delete a corner with a right-click, or delete a face by hovering it and pressing X. Removing a face relaxes a constraint; the editor refuses the removal if it would make the region unbounded.
  • S toggles snapping to the grid, and ⌘Z / ⇧⌘Z undo and redo every edit.

Faces facing away from the camera are dimmed and their edges drawn lighter, so the near side of the solid reads as near. Hovering a face highlights it, and hovering a constraint's row in the panel highlights the matching face.

The objective

The green arrow is c. Drag its tip to re-aim it, or press H to hide it when it clutters the picture. A light drop line from the tip to the ground plane is a depth cue: without it a single arrow in perspective is ambiguous. The arrow is drawn from the centroid for legibility only; the objective is a direction and the LP does not depend on where it is anchored.

Rotate Objective sweeps c over the whole sphere rather than around a circle: its azimuth advances by the angle step each frame while its inclination oscillates at an incommensurate frequency, so the directions never repeat and gradually cover every orientation. With Trace on, the solver paths from those directions accumulate into a shell around the solid; the trace keeps several passes so the shell fills in.

Solving in three variables

Every solver is written for n variables, so all five run unchanged, and the log gains a z column.

  • Simplex walks the solid's edges from vertex to vertex once Phase 1 has found a first feasible corner. Dual simplex hops between intersections of three constraint planes outside the solid, which can land far away when two of the planes are nearly parallel — the same behavior as in two variables, now in space.
  • IPM and PDHG start from the gray ring, which sits at the origin until you drag it anywhere in space (it moves on the plane facing the camera, like a corner); right-click it to reset. For primal Simplex the ring snaps to the nearest corner and the run warm-starts there.
  • The ellipsoid method draws its covering ellipsoids as wireframes — three great circles each — nesting toward the optimum. The Chebyshev, analytic and volumetric query points draw the ball or ellipsoid inscribed at each query point, and hovering a row of the log shows the polyhedron of cuts that iteration was still working in.
  • The central path runs from the solid's centroid, which is the strictly interior start its Newton steps need.

Paths are drawn as tubes with a fixed on-screen width, so a trajectory looks the same from every angle and shades like a solid; in the 2-variable app's 3D view the same applies. Hovering a log row highlights the iterate, and Animate replays the run as a sweep along the path.

Moving around

  • Orbit with a right-drag, pan by left-dragging empty space, zoom with the scroll wheel, and click the zoom button to fit the solid in view.
  • There is no height-scale slider here: z is a real coordinate, not a lifted objective value, so the solid is drawn at true scale.
  • The 2D button in the toolbar returns to the 2-variable app.

Sharing

The share button encodes the solid's corners, the objective, the chosen solver and its settings — and the start marker if you moved it — into the link, so a /3d link reproduces the exact scene. Links are plain base64url text that survives being pasted anywhere, and older 2-variable links keep working.
